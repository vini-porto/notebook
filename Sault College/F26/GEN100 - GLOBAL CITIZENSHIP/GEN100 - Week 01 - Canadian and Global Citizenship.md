# GEN100 Week 1: Canadian and Global Citizenship

Week one sets up the vocabulary for the whole course. It starts with what citizenship means, moves through how authority and government work in Canada, covers the rights and responsibilities in the [[Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms]], and ends with who votes, who doesn't, and why.

## 1. What is citizenship?

> [!note] Definition: Citizenship
> **[[Citizenship]]** is membership in a political system, along with the *rights* and *responsibilities* that come with that membership.

The rights and responsibilities both count. Being a citizen gives you things (the right to vote, for example) and asks things of you (obeying the law, for example). The lecture sums this up later as **citizenship = relationship**: a two-way connection between you and the political community.

### Global citizenship

> [!note] Definition: Global citizenship
> **[[Global Citizenship]]** is an umbrella term for the social, political, environmental, and economic actions of globally minded individuals and communities on a worldwide scale. It can also mean the belief that each person belongs to many local and non-local networks at once, instead of acting alone inside one isolated society. (United Nations, 2026)

> [!important] Key idea
> Whether we notice it or not, our actions have global effects. We are part of an interconnected global community.

> [!example] Everyday example
> The phone in your pocket probably contains minerals mined in one country, was assembled in another, and will end up as e-waste somewhere else. Buying it links you to workers, environments, and governments far from Canada.

### Citizenship vs. nationality

People often treat citizenship and nationality as the same thing. They are related, but they are different ideas.

> [!note] Definition: Nation
> A **[[Nation]]** is a group of *people* who share culture, language, history, and/or a political identity.

The word *people* is underlined on the slide for a reason: a nation is about people and shared identity, not about borders or governments.

| Idea | What it is about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Citizenship | Legal membership in a political system, with rights and responsibilities | Holding a Canadian passport and being able to vote in federal elections |
| Nation / nationality | Belonging to a group of people with shared culture, language, history or political identity | Identifying as Québécois, Cree, or Inuit |

Our identities are complex, and the way we talk about citizenship, nationality and nationhood reflects that.

### Canada: a nation made of many nations

- Canada is a nation made up of people who belong to many different nations.
- In the 2016 census, Canadians reported **over 250 ethnic origins or ancestries** (Statistics Canada, 2017).
- **Four in 10** people reported more than one origin.
- The **Québécois** and **[[Indigenous Peoples in Canada|Indigenous Peoples]]** are described as *nations within a nation*.

> [!tip] Learning tip
> When you see "nation" in this course, ask yourself whether it means a *people* (a cultural group) or a *country* (a state). The next section shows why the difference matters.

## 2. The world system: states

> [!note] Definition: State
> A **[[State (polity)|State]]** is an independent political unit with territorial boundaries that other political units recognize internationally.

- There are currently **193 [[United Nations]] member states**. ([UN member states list](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/member-states))
- The **US State Department recognizes 195** independent states. The numbers differ because recognition is a political decision, and not every country recognizes the same places.
- **Not every nation has its own state.**

> [!warning] Common mistake
> "Nation" and "state" are often used as if they mean the same thing ("the United Nations" is really an organization of *states*). A nation is a people; a state is a recognized political unit with territory. The Québécois are a nation inside the Canadian state, and they do not have a separate state of their own.

## 3. Legitimate authority

Three terms work together here:

> [!note] Definitions
> - **[[Power]]**: how we get others to do what we want. Power often relies on *coercion*, meaning sanctions and penalties.
> - **[[Sovereignty]]**: the absolute and independent authority of a nation; its right to autonomy or self-government.
> - **[[Legitimacy]]**: the moral obligation citizens feel to obey the laws and pronouncements issued by those in authority.

The lecture asks: **what is it that makes our authorities, and the laws they create, legitimate?**

In a democracy, the answers given are:

- **Electoral victories** legitimize what a government does.
- Citizens must believe that government actions are legitimate, even when some personal freedoms are limited.
- **Consent of citizens = legitimate authority.** Citizens give consent through elections, which is the idea of *[[Popular Sovereignty|popular sovereignty]]*.
- The sociologist **[[Max Weber]]** said legitimate authority rests on **tradition, charisma, and the law**.
- The Canadian government is sovereign *because* it is legitimate.

> [!example] Power vs. legitimacy
> A thief with a weapon can make you hand over your wallet. That is power through coercion. A government that collects taxes also uses penalties if you refuse, yet most people pay without being forced because they accept that the government has the right to ask. That acceptance is legitimacy.

| Weber's source of authority | What it means | Canadian example from the lecture |
|---|---|---|
| Tradition | Authority accepted because it has always been that way, often inherited | The King, whose authority is inherited |
| Charisma | Authority based on a leader's personal appeal | Popular leaders who inspire people to follow them |
| Law (legal-rational) | Authority based on rules, constitutions and elections | A Prime Minister who holds office after winning an election |

## 4. What is democracy?

> [!note] Definition: Democracy
> **[[Democracy]]** is a political system with popular sovereignty, political equality, political freedom, and majority rule.
> The word comes from Greek: *demos* (people) + *kratos* (power), so "power of the people".

- Democracy means **majority rule while protecting minority rights**. A majority vote should not be allowed to take away the basic rights of smaller groups.
- One of the basic challenges of politics and democracy is **balancing individual freedom with the public good**.

> [!example] Freedom vs. public good
> Speed limits restrict how fast you can drive (less individual freedom) to protect everyone on the road (public good). Most debates about public health rules, taxes, or protest limits are arguments about where to draw this line.

Winston Churchill's famous line on democracy:

> "Democracy is the worst form of government, except all those other forms that have been tried from time to time."

His point is that democracy has real flaws, but every alternative that has been tried has worked out worse.

More background: [Britannica: Democracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/democracy)

## 5. Who runs Canada?

Canada is a **[[Liberal Democracy|liberal democracy]]**, and its government is a **[[Constitutional Monarchy|constitutional monarchy]]**. That means there is a monarch, but the monarch rules according to the constitution, and real political decisions are made by elected officials.

### Head of state: the King

- Title: **King**. Name: **Charles III**.
- Holds *ultimate authority* in theory.
- Rules according to the constitution.
- His authority is **inherited** (a clear case of Weber's *tradition*).
- His role is mostly **symbolic and ceremonial**.

### Head of state in Canada: the Governor General

- Title: **[[Governor General of Canada|Governor General]]**. The person shown on the slide is **Mary Simon**.
- Appointed on the Prime Minister's recommendation (formally, the King makes the appointment on the PM's advice).
- **Represents the King in Canada.**
- Has the highest level of political responsibility. For example, the Governor General gives **[[Royal Assent]]**, the final step for a federal bill to become law.
- In practice, the role is also mostly symbolic and ceremonial.

### Head of government: the Prime Minister

- Title: **[[Prime Minister of Canada|Prime Minister]]**. The person shown on the slide is **Mark Carney**.
- Controls federal policy making, the government, and the legislative agenda.
- **Elected.**
- Is the leader of the party that wins the most seats (also called districts, ridings or constituencies) in a national election.

| Role | Person (per slides) | How they get the job | Real power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head of state | King Charles III | Inherited | Mostly symbolic and ceremonial |
| King's representative in Canada | Governor General Mary Simon | Appointed on the PM's advice | Formal duties such as Royal Assent; mostly ceremonial |
| Head of government | Prime Minister Mark Carney | Leads the party that wins the most seats | Runs the government and sets policy |

> [!warning] Common mistake
> The King is the head of *state*, not the head of *government*. The person who actually runs the federal government day to day is the Prime Minister.

## 6. Parliament

### Political parties and party discipline

There are nearly **42 million Canadians**. They agree on many issues and disagree on others. Political parties are how people with similar views organize.

> [!note] Definition: Political party
> **[[Political Parties|Political parties]]** are (a) groups of Canadians with similar beliefs about what government should or should not do (these beliefs form the party's **[[Ideology|ideology]]**), and (b) groups that nominate candidates and contest elections, hoping to influence who is in government and what policies it adopts.

- You do **not** have to belong to a party to run for office. A candidate without a party is an **independent**.

> [!note] Definition: Party discipline
> **[[Party Discipline|Party discipline]]** means that once elected, members of each party promote the same policies and vote together on almost every occasion, like a team.

### What "Parliament" means

> [!note] Definition: Parliament
> In theory, **[[Parliament of Canada|Parliament]]** is the King, the House of Commons, and the Senate working together, for example when approving legislation. In everyday use, "Parliament" often means just the House of Commons, or sometimes the Commons and the Senate.

The **[[House of Commons of Canada|House of Commons]]** is the part most people think of when they hear "Parliament", and it is by far the most important part:

- It holds our elected officials, the people's representatives.
- Its members are democratically elected.
- There are **343 Members of Parliament (MPs)**. Each represents one constituency (riding), so all regions of Canada are represented.

### The Westminster model

Canada uses the **[[Westminster System|Westminster model]]** of parliamentary government (named after the UK Parliament at Westminster).

1. The **government** is chosen based on which party leader can keep the **"confidence"** (a majority of votes) of the House of Commons.
2. That leader becomes **Prime Minister** and chooses the **[[Cabinet of Canada|Cabinet]]**, usually other members of the PM's party.
3. The PM relies on **party discipline** to win votes and govern.
4. An **institutionalized opposition** criticizes the government.
5. The result is meant to be a **stable and accountable** form of government.

> [!tip] Learning tip
> Connect the pieces: party discipline is what lets the PM keep the confidence of the House. If MPs voted however they liked, the government could lose a key vote at any time.

## 7. Values

> [!note] Definition: Values
> **[[Values]]** are beliefs about ideal goals and behaviour that serve as standards for social life. They are culturally defined standards of what is good, evil, beautiful, desirable, moral, and so on.

- Values are one of the building blocks of **[[Culture|culture]]**.
- Values are not tangible or physical objects. You can't hold "justice" in your hand.
- Cultural groups may share values.
- Cultural groups may also differ in their values, or in how those values show up in practice.

### "Western" values

Some values that are often called "Western":

- Equality
- Justice
- Security
- Freedom
- Liberty
- Social order
- Peace
- Democracy

> [!warning] Common misconception
> These values are **not unique to the Western world**. Many cultures hold them, even if they express them in different ways.

The lecture then asks: **are there such a thing as Canadian values?**

### Distinctly Canadian values (compared to the USA)

Compared to Americans, Canadians tend to show:

| Canadian tendency | What it means in simple terms |
|---|---|
| Closer balance between individualism and collectivism | More weight on the group and shared responsibility, alongside personal freedom |
| Value diversity; more tolerant of minorities | Differences are accepted and protected |
| More deferential toward authority | More trust in, and willingness to follow, institutions and leaders |
| Stronger belief in egalitarianism | Stronger support for social and political equality |
| More cautious and less violent | Less risk-taking, lower levels of violence |

> [!example] Collectivism in practice
> Publicly funded health care is often given as an example of the Canadian balance toward collectivism: everyone pays in through taxes so that everyone can get care.

## 8. What does it mean to be a Canadian citizen?

Back to the definition: citizenship is membership with **RIGHTS** and **RESPONSIBILITIES**. The lecture covers each side.

### Your rights: the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

The **[[Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms]]** lists the rights protected in Canada. Full text: [Justice Laws Website: the Charter](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/page-12.html)

| Category | Sections | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental freedoms | 2 | Conscience and religion; thought, belief, opinion and expression (including the press and other media); peaceful assembly; association |
| Democratic rights | 3 to 5 | The right to vote; a maximum of 5 years between elections (with exceptions); Parliament must sit at least once every 12 months |
| Mobility rights | 6 | Enter, remain in and leave Canada; move to and live in any province; earn a living in any province |
| Legal rights | 7 to 14 | Life, liberty and security of the person; protection from unreasonable search or seizure; no arbitrary detention; no cruel and unusual punishment; protection against self-incrimination; right to an interpreter |
| Equality rights | 15 (and 28) | Equal protection and benefit of the law without discrimination |
| Language rights | 16 to 23 | English and French as official languages |
| Indigenous rights | 25 and 35 | Existing Indigenous and treaty rights recognized and affirmed |

#### Fundamental freedoms (Section 2)

- Freedom of conscience and religion
- Freedom of thought, belief, opinion and expression, including freedom of the press and other media of communication
- Freedom of peaceful assembly
- Freedom of association

#### Democratic rights (Sections 3 to 5)

- The right to vote.
- A Parliament can go at most **5 years** between elections (with some exceptions).
- Parliament must sit **at least once every 12 months**.

#### Mobility rights (Section 6)

- Every Canadian citizen can enter, remain in, and leave Canada.
- Citizens can move to and live in any province.
- Citizens can pursue a livelihood (earn a living) in any province.

#### Legal rights (Sections 7 to 14)

- The right to **life, liberty and security of the person**, and the right not to lose these except in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.
- Protection against **unreasonable search or seizure**. This includes the right not to give blood or DNA, or be strip searched, without a warrant.
- The right not to be **arbitrarily detained or imprisoned**.
- The right not to face **cruel and/or unusual treatment or punishment**.
- If your testimony as a witness incriminates you, that evidence **cannot be used against you**, except in a prosecution for perjury or for giving contradictory evidence.
- The right to the **assistance of an interpreter**.

#### Equality rights (Section 15)

- Every individual is equal under the law and has the right to equal protection and equal benefit of the law **without discrimination**.
- It prohibits discrimination based on race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, or mental or physical disability.
- Courts have interpreted it to include **sexual orientation**, even though the text doesn't say so explicitly. This interpretation led to **same-sex marriage** in Canada.
- It allows laws whose goal is to reduce existing discrimination and historical disadvantage.

> [!note] Definition: Affirmative action
> **[[Affirmative Action|Affirmative action]]** is a law or program that gives preference to individuals with certain characteristics in hiring or promotion, in order to address historical disadvantage. Section 15 allows these programs.

- **Gender equality** is protected again in **Section 28**, and Section 28 is **immune to the notwithstanding clause**.

> [!tip] What is the notwithstanding clause?
> The **[[Notwithstanding Clause|notwithstanding clause]]** (Section 33 of the Charter) lets Parliament or a provincial legislature pass a law that overrides certain Charter rights for a limited time. Saying gender equality is "immune" means governments cannot use that clause to override it.

#### Language rights (Sections 16 to 23)

- **English and French** are Canada's official languages. They have equal status, rights and privileges in all institutions of Parliament and the Government of Canada.
- Certain government agencies must operate on a **bilingual** basis.
- Any member of the public can communicate with, and receive available services from, any federal government agency or institution in English or French.
- Education is a provincial responsibility, but the Charter guarantees that minority languages (French or English) are taught **where local numbers warrant**.

> [!example] Language rights in practice
> A French-speaking family living in Sault Ste. Marie can access French-language schooling for their children where there are enough students to justify it, even though Ontario is mostly English speaking.

#### Indigenous rights (Sections 25 and 35)

- The Constitution recognizes three distinct groups of Indigenous (Aboriginal) Peoples: **[[First Nations]], [[Métis]] and [[Inuit]]**.
- Existing Indigenous and treaty rights are **recognized and affirmed**.
- The Charter **cannot be interpreted to remove or reduce** any treaty or other rights and freedoms of Indigenous peoples.
- It guarantees treaty rights, land claims settlements, and any other rights gained by Indigenous Peoples; the Charter cannot be used to override them.

### Your responsibilities as a citizen

- Understand and obey Canada's laws
- Express opinions freely while respecting the rights and freedoms of others
- Help others in the community
- Care for and protect our heritage and environment
- Eliminate discrimination and injustice
- Vote in elections (municipal, provincial and federal)
- Support Canada's ideals in building the country

> [!important] Key idea
> **Citizenship = relationship.** The state protects your rights, and you take on responsibilities in return.

## 9. Political participation

> [!note] Definition: Political participation
> **[[Political Participation|Political participation]]** is the voluntary activities by citizens meant to influence who is chosen as a government leader or the decisions those leaders make. Put more simply: any action taken to influence government policy.

- Voting is **one aspect** of political participation.
- Political participation takes two forms: **electoral** and **non-electoral**.
- Voting is the most common form of electoral participation, but on average it has **decreased since 1988**.

> [!note] Definition: Political efficacy
> **[[Political Efficacy|Political efficacy]]** is the feeling that you have political influence and that your participation can make a difference.

### Electoral vs. non-electoral participation

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Electoral | Voting | Voting in a municipal, provincial or federal election |
| Non-electoral | Voluntary activities other than voting that aim to influence leaders or their decisions | Joining or volunteering for a political party; volunteering for advocacy or interest groups; demonstrations, protests and marches; signing petitions or writing letters; contacting your MP, the media or the government; boycotts and sit-ins; town halls; running for office; raising issues in organizations you already belong to; engaging through social media; using the website [Vote Compass](https://votecompass.cbc.ca) |

> [!important] Key idea
> **Knowledge is the first step in participating politically.** You can't influence a system you don't understand.

### Voter turnout is falling

Voting is a crucial part of democracy, but for decades turnout has trended downward. Federal election turnout (Elections Canada):

| Federal election | Turnout |
|---|---|
| 2006 | 64.7% |
| 2008 | 58.8% (all-time low) |
| 2011 | 61.1% |
| 2015 | 68.3% |
| 2019 | 67.0% |
| 2021 | 62.5% |
| 2025 | 68.65% |

This raises questions about the **health of our democracy** and the **legitimacy** of a particular government. If fewer people vote, can a government still claim the consent of the citizens? (See section 3.)

**Chart: Voter turnout in Canadian federal elections, 1867 to 2025** (Elections Canada via CBC). The line stays mostly between about 63% and 80% from Confederation until the late 1980s, peaking near 80% around the 1950s and 1960s. It then drops sharply through the 1990s and 2000s to a low just under 60% in 2008, recovers to around 68% in 2015, dips again in 2021, and ends at **68.47%** for 2025. The chart notes that the 2025 figure did not yet include electors who registered on election day, which is why it is slightly lower than the 68.65% on the earlier slide.

**Chart: Voter turnout in Ontario provincial elections, 1867 to 2022** (Elections Ontario, Toronto Star graphic). Ontario follows a similar downward trend, with peaks above 80% around 1900 and 1920. Turnout falls through the 2000s to about 48% around 2011, climbs to about 57% in 2018, then drops to **43% in 2022, the province's lowest ever**. A later point on the chart sits slightly higher than 2022, though its label is cut off on the slide.

More data: [Elections Canada: voter turnout](https://www.elections.ca/content.aspx?section=ele&dir=turn&document=index&lang=e)

### Who votes and who doesn't

| Tend to vote | Tend not to vote |
|---|---|
| Wealthy | Alienated |
| Educated | Poor |
| Elderly | Young |
| | Working class |

Canadian voter turnout rates have been declining for decades.

> [!warning] Why this matters
> If some groups vote much less than others, governments may pay more attention to the groups that do vote. The concerns of young, poor or working-class Canadians can end up underrepresented.

### Why don't people vote?

**Traditional reasons:**

- Lack of interest
- Unappealing alternatives (choosing the lesser evil)
- Weak party identification
- Feeling that government is irrelevant or not listening
- Feeling that voting for a minor party wastes a vote
- Lack of time
- Believing the result is already decided

**2021 federal election (Statistics Canada):**

| Reason for not voting | Share of non-voters |
|---|---|
| Everyday life or health reasons | 43% |
| Too busy (part of the above) | 24% |
| Illness or disability (part of the above) | 11% |
| Out of town (part of the above) | 9% |
| Political reasons | 39% |
| Not interested in politics (part of the above) | 32% |
| Electoral process-related reasons | 7% |
| COVID-19 | 2% |
| All other reasons | 8% |

> [!tip] Reading the table
> The indented categories are parts of the bigger categories. "Too busy", "out of town" and "illness or disability" add up to the 43% for everyday life or health reasons, and "not interested in politics" is most of the 39% for political reasons.

> This links back to political efficacy and to "knowledge is the first step". A young person who doesn't understand how Parliament works, and doesn't believe their vote matters, is less likely to show up on election day.

## 10. Political definitions of liberty

| | Negative liberty | Positive liberty |
|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Freedom *from* interference | Freedom *to* reach your potential |
| Government's role | Stays out; leaves you alone | Steps in to provide opportunities |
| Linked to | The idea of having rights | More equal opportunities to succeed |
| Example | Choosing your religion without government interference | Government providing infrastructure, education, or new ways of distributing resources |

> [!note] Definitions
> - **[[Negative Liberty|Negative liberty]]**: areas of activity the government does NOT interfere in. Individuals are free to choose, for example their religion. This ties into the idea of having rights.
> - **[[Positive Liberty|Positive liberty]]**: the freedom to reach your potential. The government provides more equal opportunities to succeed by intervening with infrastructure, education, and/or new means of distribution.

The slide asks for examples of positive liberty. Public schools, student loans and grants, and public transit fit the definition, since each is government action that helps people do things they otherwise couldn't.

> [!tip] Connect it to the Charter
> Most of the Charter's fundamental freedoms (Section 2) are negative liberties: they stop the government from interfering. Programs allowed under Section 15, such as affirmative action, lean toward positive liberty because the government acts to create more equal opportunities.

> - **King Charles III**: head of state; authority is **inherited**; role mostly symbolic and ceremonial.
> - **Mary Simon** (Governor General, per the slides): represents the King in Canada; **appointed** on the Prime Minister's advice; gives Royal Assent, but the role is also mostly ceremonial.
> - **Mark Carney** (Prime Minister, per the slides): head of government; leader of the party that won the most seats, so his authority comes from an **election**. He controls policy and the legislative agenda.
>
> The pattern shows a constitutional monarchy at work: the inherited and appointed roles are mostly symbolic, and the elected role holds the real power.

### Exercise 4 (Applied variation)

After a federal election, Party A holds 160 of the 343 seats, Party B holds 120, and three smaller parties hold the rest. Using the Westminster model, explain who is likely to become Prime Minister, what that person needs to stay in power, and why party discipline matters in this situation.

> [!success]- Answer key
> In the Westminster model, the government is formed by the party leader who can keep the **confidence** (a majority of votes) of the House of Commons. A majority of 343 is 172 seats.
> - Party A's leader, with the most seats, is the most likely to become **Prime Minister**, but 160 is short of 172. To stay in power, the PM needs support from at least 12 MPs from other parties on key votes.
> - **Party discipline** is essential here. The PM needs all 160 Party A MPs to vote as a team; if even a few broke ranks, the government could lose a confidence vote.
> - The **institutionalized opposition** (led by Party B) will criticize the government, which is part of what makes the system accountable.
> - This links to legitimacy: the PM's authority comes from elections (consent of citizens) and from keeping the House's confidence.

### Exercise 5 (Applied variation)

A province's election has a turnout of 43%, and the winning party receives about 41% of the votes cast. Using the ideas of **legitimacy**, **consent of citizens** and **who does and doesn't vote**, explain why some people question how legitimate this government is. Then give one counterargument.

> [!success]- Answer key
> 41% of 43% is roughly 18% of all eligible voters. Critics can argue:
> - The lecture says **consent of citizens = legitimate authority**, given through elections. If fewer than half of eligible voters take part, the government's claim to have the people's consent looks weaker.
> - Turnout is uneven. The **wealthy, educated and elderly** vote more, while the **poor, young, alienated and working class** vote less. The result may reflect some groups much more than others.
> - The lecture says falling turnout "raises questions about the health of our democracy and the legitimacy of a particular government."
>
> **Counterargument:** The election followed the law, and everyone had the right to vote (Charter Sections 3 to 5). In Weber's terms the government still has *legal* authority, and by not voting, non-voters accepted the outcome chosen by those who did.
>
> (The 43% figure matches Ontario's 2022 provincial election, its lowest turnout ever.)

### Exercise 6 (Applied variation)

Classify each policy as closer to **negative liberty** or **positive liberty**, and explain why:
a) The government passes no law about which religion people may follow.
b) The government funds free public colleges.
c) The government builds broadband internet in remote northern communities.
d) The government does not require citizens to join any political party.

> [!success]- Answer key
> a) **Negative liberty.** The government stays out of the decision, and individuals choose freely. This is the lecture's own example (religion).
> b) **Positive liberty.** The government intervenes through education so people have more equal opportunities to reach their potential.
> c) **Positive liberty.** Building infrastructure is named in the definition. It gives people in remote areas opportunities they otherwise wouldn't have.
> d) **Negative liberty.** The government leaves people alone, which connects to the freedom of association in Section 2.

### Exercise 7 (Challenge)

A student says: "Canada is a nation, so it's the same thing as a state, and everyone in it shares one national identity." Use at least four ideas from this lecture to explain what is wrong with that statement.

> [!success]- Answer key
> 1. **Nation and state are different.** A nation is a *people* who share culture, language, history and/or political identity. A state is an independent political unit with internationally recognized borders.
> 2. **Not every nation has its own state.** The Québécois and Indigenous Peoples are "nations within a nation" inside the Canadian state.
> 3. **Canada is diverse.** In 2016, Canadians reported over 250 ethnic origins, and 4 in 10 reported more than one origin. Many Canadians hold several identities at once.
> 4. **Citizenship differs from nationality.** Citizenship is legal membership with rights and responsibilities, so two people can share citizenship and belong to different nations.
> 5. **The Constitution reflects this.** It recognizes First Nations, Métis and Inuit as distinct peoples (Sections 25 and 35) and protects two official languages (Sections 16 to 23).
>
> A better statement: Canada is a state whose citizens belong to many different nations and hold complex, overlapping identities.

### Exercise 8 (Challenge)

Your college wants to raise youth turnout in the next federal election. Using the lecture's data and reasons for not voting, design a three-part plan. For each part, say which barrier it targets and which lecture concept supports it.

Concept: youth who volunteer are usually the same ones who vote, so non-electoral participation can lead to electoral participation.
>
