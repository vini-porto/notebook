# GEN100 Week 3: Social Problems and Social Change

This week looks at how people try to fix social problems. The lecture starts with what [[Social Change|social change]] is, then moves from small to large: what individuals do with family and friends (micro level), what self-help and grassroots groups do (mid-range), and what [[Social Movements|social movements]] and [[Special Interest Groups|special interest groups]] do to pressure governments and other big institutions (macro level). It ends with the five types of national and international social movements, plus the group work and Reflection One details.

## 1. Social change and the sociological imagination

> [!note] Definition: Social change
> **[[Social Change]]** is the alteration, modification, or transformation of public policy, culture, or social institutions over time (Kendall, 2000).

Three kinds of things can change here:

- **Public policy**: laws and government programs (for example, a new law on impaired driving).
- **Culture**: shared values, beliefs and habits (for example, how people view mental illness).
- **Social institutions**: the big structures of society such as family, education, the economy, government, and media.

The key words are *over time*. Social change is usually gradual and happens because people push for it.

> [!note] Definition: Sociological imagination
> The **[[Sociological Imagination]]** is the ability to see the relationship between an individual's experiences and the larger society.

The term comes from the sociologist [[C. Wright Mills]]. It asks you to look at a personal situation and see the social forces around it.

> [!example] Sociological imagination in practice
> One person losing their job is a personal problem. If thousands of people in the same town lose their jobs in the same year because a mill closes, the cause is no longer about any one person's effort. Seeing that link between the one person and the wider economy is the sociological imagination.

> [!important] Key idea
> The sociological imagination is the reason this lecture exists. If social problems were only personal, individuals could solve them alone. Because they are connected to society, solving them often needs groups, organizations and institutions.

## 2. Three levels of social context

The slide shows three circles inside each other:

| Level | Where it sits in the diagram | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Micro** | Small inner circle | Individuals and the small groups around them |
| **Macro** | Middle circle | Large institutions within a society, such as government and media |
| **Global** | Large outer circle | The whole world, across countries |

Each circle sits inside the next one. What happens in your personal life (micro) is shaped by your country's institutions (macro), which are shaped by what happens around the world (global).

> [!tip] Learning tip
> The rest of the lecture walks through the levels in order: micro solutions, then **mid-range** solutions (a step between micro and macro that is not drawn in the diagram), then macro solutions. Movements that cross borders bring in the global level.

## 3. Micro level attempts to solve social problems

The lecture starts with a limit: **social problems cannot be solved at the individual level**, because they are more than personal troubles or private problems.

So why do people still start at the individual level? The slides explain:

- Sometimes **social institutions cannot deal with a problem effectively**.
- Political and business leaders may be **unwilling or unable to allocate the resources** needed to deal with the issue.
- In these situations, people typically begin to deal with the problem in an **individualized way**, on their own or with those closest to them.

### Micro level and primary groups

> [!note] Definition: Micro level solutions
> **Micro level** solutions to social problems focus on how individuals operate within small groups.

When people have personal problems, they usually turn to their **primary group**. This often looks like **short-term support**.

> [!note] Definition: Primary group
> **[[Primary Groups|Primary groups]]** are small, less-specialized groups in which members engage in face-to-face, emotion-based interactions over an extended period of time (Kendall, 2000).
> Examples: family, close friends, and other peers with whom one routinely shares the more personal experiences in life.

"Less specialized" means the group does not exist for one specific task. Your family is not organized around a single activity; it is there for many parts of your life.

### Critical thinking: what does this look like in our society?

The slide asks for examples of micro level responses. Some that fit the definition:

> [!example] Micro level examples
> - A student who can't afford rent moves back in with their parents for a semester.
> - A friend struggling with anxiety talks to a close friend or sibling late at night instead of seeing a professional.
> - Family members take turns caring for an elderly grandparent because long-term care has a long waitlist.
> - Someone dealing with heavy drinking is confronted by their partner and family.
>
> In each case, the support is personal, face to face, and often short term. It helps the person cope, but it doesn't change the rent market, the mental health system or long-term care funding.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Micro level help is real and important, but it does not remove the cause of a social problem. Family can help one person pay rent; it cannot lower rent for everyone. That gap is why the lecture moves on to larger levels.

## 4. Mid-range attempts to solve social problems

> [!note] Definition: Mid-range solutions
> **Mid-range** solutions focus on how **secondary groups** and formal organizations can help individuals overcome issues such as drug addiction or domestic violence.

> [!note] Definition: Secondary group
> **[[Secondary Groups|Secondary groups]]** are more specialized, activity-oriented groups that have defined roles and expectations.

| | Primary group | Secondary group |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Small | Can be larger |
| Purpose | General, less specialized | Specific activity or goal |
| Relationships | Emotional, personal, face to face | More formal, with defined roles and expectations |
| Time | Long lasting | Often tied to the goal or activity |
| Examples | Family, close friends | A support group, a community organization, a sports team, a college class |

There are two kinds of mid-range groups:

1. Groups that help people **cope with their own problems** (self-help groups).
2. Groups that try to bring about **community change** (grassroots groups).

### Self-help groups

The best-known examples are **[[Alcoholics Anonymous]] (AA)** and **[[Narcotics Anonymous]] (NA)**. The slide shows both logos; the AA logo is a triangle in a circle with the words *Unity*, *Service* and *Recovery*.

How self-help groups work:

- They bring together individuals who have **experienced the same problem** and have **the same goals**.
- **Volunteers who have had similar problems** often act as role models for newer members. In AA, for example, a longer-term member can act as a sponsor for a newcomer.
- **Social interaction** is seen as central to each person's success in the program.

The last point on the slide is the important one: **often a larger societal intervention is necessary** to reduce the problems that contribute to individual behaviours.

> [!important] Key idea
> Self-help groups help individuals cope, one person at a time. They don't change the conditions that create the problem in the first place (for example poverty, trauma, or the easy availability of drugs). That requires action at a bigger scale.

More about these groups: [Alcoholics Anonymous](https://www.aa.org) and [Narcotics Anonymous](https://na.org).

### Grassroots groups that work for community-based change

Some grassroots organizations focus on bringing about a change that may **reduce or eliminate a social problem in a specific community or region**.

> [!note] Definition: Grassroots groups
> **[[Grassroots Organizations|Grassroots groups]]** are organizations started by ordinary people who work to deal with a perceived problem in their neighbourhood, city, province or territory, or nation.

With this approach, people learn how to **empower themselves** against:

- local, provincial, territorial and national government officials
- corporate executives
- media figures who decide what counts as "news" in their area

> [!example] Grassroots group
> Parents in a neighbourhood notice that cars speed past the local elementary school. They form a residents' group, collect signatures, show up at city council meetings, and invite local reporters to cover the issue. After months of pressure, the city installs a crosswalk and speed bumps. Nobody in the group was a politician or an expert; they were ordinary people who learned how to get the attention of decision makers.

| | Self-help group | Grassroots group |
|---|---|---|
| Main goal | Help members cope with their own problem | Change something in the community |
| Focus | The individual | The community or region |
| Example | AA, NA | A neighbourhood group pushing for safer streets |

### The deeper issue

The lecture quotes this passage:

> "The crisis is that we as a people don't know how to come together to solve these problems. We lack the capacities to address the issues or remove the obstacles that stand in the way of public deliberation. **TOO MANY PEOPLE FEEL POWERLESS.**"

*Public deliberation* means people discussing problems together and deciding what to do about them. The quote says the biggest obstacle is that people believe they can't make a difference, so they never organize in the first place. Grassroots groups respond to this by teaching people that they can.

> [!tip] Connect it to Week 1
> This links to **[[Political Efficacy|political efficacy]]** from Week 1: the feeling that your participation can make a difference. People who feel powerless have low political efficacy.

## 5. Social movements

> [!note] Definition: Social movement
> A **[[Social Movements|social movement]]** is an organized group that acts collectively to **promote or resist change** through collective action.

Key features from the slides (Kendall, 2000):

- When they begin, social movements are **not institutionalized**. They are not part of government or of established organizations.
- They are **outside the political mainstream** at the start.
- They **empower outsiders** by giving them an opportunity to have their voices heard.

Examples from the slides:

| Movement | What it works on |
|---|---|
| **[[Greenpeace]]** | Environmental protection ([Greenpeace Canada](https://www.greenpeace.org/canada/en/)) |
| **[[Mothers Against Drunk Driving]] (MADD)** | Stopping impaired driving and supporting victims ([MADD Canada](https://madd.ca)) |
| **Canadian Peace Alliance** | Peace and anti-war activism in Canada |

> [!warning] Common mistake
> A social movement can **resist** change as well as promote it. A movement that tries to stop or reverse a change is still a social movement. (See resistance movements in section 8.)

More background: [Britannica: Social movement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-movement)

## 6. Macro level attempts to solve social problems

> [!note] Definition: Macro level solutions
> **Macro level** ("the big picture") solutions focus on how **large-scale social institutions**, such as **the government and the media**, can be persuaded to get involved in remedying social problems.

The slide gives the reasoning in three steps:

1. **Many individuals feel powerless** on their own.
2. But **millions of people are affected in the same way**.
3. So they **bind together in organizations to pressure those who make national and global decisions**.

> [!example] Macro level thinking
> One person paying high grocery prices can only shop around. Millions of people facing the same prices can organize, contact MPs, attract media coverage, and push for government action on food prices. The problem is the same; the scale of the response is different.

### Special interest / pressure groups

> [!note] Definition: Special interest group
> A **[[Special Interest Groups|special interest group]]** (also called a **pressure group**) is a political coalition made up of individuals or groups who share a specific interest that they want to protect or advance with the help of the political system.

Examples from the slides:

- Unions
- Canadian Association of Petroleum Producers
- Native Women's Association of Canada
- Canadian AIDS Society
- Canadian Medical Association
- Canadian Council of Retailers

The list mixes very different interests: workers, an industry, Indigenous women, people living with HIV/AIDS, doctors and retailers. What they share is the method: they work through the political system to protect or advance their interest.

### Categorizing special interest / pressure groups

The lecture gives four ways to sort these groups. (The slides number the last ones "3" twice; there are four categories in total.)

| # | Category | Question it asks | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Issue focus** | How many issues does the group work on? | A single issue, or multiple issues with a similar focus |
| 2 | **Views of the present system of wealth and power** | How much does the group want to change the system? | Radical demands to end patriarchy, capitalism, governmental bureaucracy or other power structures, or just specific social reforms |
| 3 | **Beliefs about elites** | How does the group relate to people in power? | Some want to *influence* elites; others want to *replace* them altogether |
| 4 | **Type of political action** | How does the group act? | In recent decades, many have been single-issue groups that focus on electing and endorsing politicians who support their views |

> [!example] Using the categories
> MADD focuses on one issue (impaired driving), wants specific reforms (stricter laws) rather than an end to the whole system, and tries to influence lawmakers rather than replace them. A group calling for the end of capitalism sits at the radical end of category 2 and probably wants to replace elites (category 3).

## 7. Working through national social movements to reduce problems

The slide separates two ways large numbers of people act together.

> [!note] Definition: Collective behaviour
> **[[Collective Behaviour|Collective behaviour]]** is voluntary, often spontaneous activity by a large number of people that typically violates dominant group norms and values (Kendall, 2000).
> Examples: public demonstrations and riots.

> [!note] Definition: Civil disobedience
> **[[Civil Disobedience|Civil disobedience]]** is non-violent action that seeks to change a policy or law by refusing to comply with it.
> Examples: sit-ins, strikes, marches, boycotts.

| | Collective behaviour | Civil disobedience |
|---|---|---|
| Planning | Often spontaneous | Usually planned and organized |
| Violence | Can include violence (riots) | Non-violent by definition |
| Goal | Not always a clear goal | Change a specific policy or law |
| Relationship to norms or law | Breaks dominant norms and values | Openly refuses to obey a law or policy |
| Examples | Demonstrations, riots | Sit-ins, strikes, marches, boycotts |

More background: [Britannica: Civil disobedience](https://www.britannica.com/topic/civil-disobedience)

### Discussion question: the "COVID Convoy"

The slide asks where the "COVID Convoy" from the pandemic would fit.

Some context: in early 2022, the protest often called the Freedom Convoy brought truckers and supporters to Ottawa to oppose COVID-19 vaccine mandates and other public health rules. Protesters occupied downtown Ottawa for about three weeks, and related blockades closed border crossings such as the Ambassador Bridge in Windsor. The federal government invoked the *Emergencies Act* for the first time to end it.

There isn't one correct answer, which is the point of the question. Arguments for each side:

- **Civil disobedience:** it was organized, aimed at changing specific policies (the mandates), and participants refused to comply with orders to leave.
- **Collective behaviour:** it grew partly spontaneously, drew a very large number of people, and violated dominant norms (constant horn honking, blocking streets and border crossings for weeks).
- It can also be described as a **resistance movement** (section 8), because it tried to undo changes that had already happened.

> [!tip] How to answer this kind of question
> Pick a category, then defend it with the parts of the definition that fit and admit the parts that don't. For example, civil disobedience requires non-violent action, so you would need to discuss whether the convoy's tactics stayed within that.

## 8. Types of national and international social movements

The lecture lists five types. (Again, the slides repeat the number 3; there are five in total.)

| # | Type | Goal | Examples from the slides |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **[[Reform Movements|Reform movements]]** | Improve society by changing **some specific aspect** of the social structure | Environmental groups, disability rights groups |
| 2 | **[[Revolutionary Movements|Revolutionary movements]]** | Bring about a **total change** in society | Utopian groups, radical terrorist groups |
| 3 | **Religious / expressive movements** | Renew people through **inner change**; often also try to persuade officials to pass laws that reduce perceived social problems | (none given) |
| 4 | **Alternative movements** | Seek **limited change** in some aspects of **people's behaviour** | (none given) |
| 5 | **[[Resistance Movements|Resistance movements]]** | **Prevent or undo** change that has already occurred | Opposition to same-sex marriage, anti-abortion groups |

> [!tip] How to tell them apart
> Ask two questions:
> 1. **What is being changed?** Society's structure (reform, revolutionary), people's inner selves (religious/expressive), or people's behaviour (alternative)?
> 2. **How much, and in which direction?** A specific part (reform), everything (revolutionary), a limited part (alternative), or backward, to stop or undo change (resistance)?

> [!example] Examples for the types without slide examples
> - **Alternative movement:** a campaign encouraging people to quit smoking or cut down on single-use plastic. It asks individuals to change one habit, not society as a whole.
> - **Religious/expressive movement:** a faith-based group that encourages members to change their lives through spiritual renewal and also lobbies government on a moral issue.

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Reform and revolutionary movements both want change. The difference is scale: reform movements want to fix **one part** of the system and keep the rest, while revolutionary movements want to replace the **whole** system. Resistance movements are the only type that pushes against change.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1 (Direct application)

Classify each response as **micro**, **mid-range** or **macro** level:
a) A woman leaving an abusive relationship stays with her sister for a few weeks.
b) A man attends weekly Narcotics Anonymous meetings.
c) A national coalition of health groups lobbies Parliament to fund supervised consumption sites.
d) A teenager talks to their best friend about feeling depressed.
e) A local shelter runs a program that helps women find housing after leaving abusive partners.

> [!success]- Answer key
> a) **Micro.** The sister is part of her primary group, and the support is short term and personal.
> b) **Mid-range.** NA is a self-help group, a secondary group that helps members cope with their own problem.
> c) **Macro.** The coalition is trying to persuade a large-scale institution (the government) to act on a problem that affects many people.
> d) **Micro.** A best friend is a primary group member; the interaction is face to face and emotion based.
> e) **Mid-range.** A shelter is a formal organization helping individuals overcome an issue like domestic violence, which is the lecture's own example of mid-range help.

### Exercise 2 (Direct application)

Name the type of social movement (reform, revolutionary, religious/expressive, alternative, or resistance) for each:
a) A group that wants to overturn a law allowing same-sex marriage.
b) A disability rights group pushing for all public buildings to be wheelchair accessible.
c) A group that wants to abolish the current government and economy and build a completely new society.
d) A campaign encouraging people to reduce their meat consumption one day a week.
e) A faith-based movement that urges members to renew their spiritual lives and lobbies government on moral issues.

> [!success]- Answer key
> a) **Resistance.** It wants to undo a change that has already happened. The lecture uses opposition to same-sex marriage as an example.
> b) **Reform.** It wants to change one specific part of the social structure (accessibility). Disability rights groups are a slide example.
> c) **Revolutionary.** It seeks total change in society.
> d) **Alternative.** It asks for limited change in one part of people's behaviour.
> e) **Religious/expressive.** It focuses on inner change, and the lecture notes these movements often also try to get officials to pass laws.

### Exercise 3 (Direct application)

Decide whether each is **collective behaviour** or **civil disobedience**, and explain using the definitions:
a) After a hockey team loses a final, fans flood downtown streets, smash windows and set cars on fire.
b) Students occupy the college president's office and refuse to leave until tuition increases are cancelled.
c) Workers walk off the job and refuse to return until a new contract is signed.

> [!success]- Answer key

> b) **Civil disobedience.** It is a non-violent sit-in aimed at changing a specific policy by refusing to comply (refusing to leave).
> c) **Civil disobedience.** Strikes are listed as an example: non-violent refusal aimed at changing a specific condition.

### Exercise 4 (Applied variation)

Choose the Canadian Association of Petroleum Producers and the Native Women's Association of Canada. Using the four categories for special interest groups, describe where each one would likely fall. Explain your reasoning for each category.

> [!success]- Answer key
> Answers can vary, but should use all four categories:
> - **Issue focus:** both work on multiple issues with a similar focus. For petroleum producers, that is the oil and gas industry; for the Native Women's Association, the well-being and rights of Indigenous women.
> - **Views of the present system of wealth and power:** petroleum producers benefit from the current system and want specific policies (for example on regulation or pipelines), not an end to capitalism. The Native Women's Association pushes for significant reforms on issues affecting Indigenous women, though as a national organization it works within the political system.
> - **Beliefs about elites:** both try to **influence** elites (ministers, MPs, officials) rather than replace them.
> - **Type of political action:** both lobby governments and try to shape policy through the political system, which matches the definition of a special interest group.
>
> The exercise shows that very different groups can use the same method: working through the political system to protect or advance a specific interest.

### Exercise 5 (Applied variation)

A town has a growing problem with youth homelessness. For each level, describe one realistic response and one limitation:
a) Micro level
b) Mid-range level (self-help or grassroots)
c) Macro level

> [!success]- Answer key
> a) **Micro:** a youth couch-surfs with friends or relatives (primary group). *Limitation:* short-term support only; it does nothing about the lack of affordable housing or the reasons youth leave home.
> b) **Mid-range:** residents form a grassroots group that raises money and pressures city council to open a youth shelter. *Limitation:* it can help one community, but the group's power is limited if provincial or federal funding is needed.
> c) **Macro:** a national coalition and the media push the federal and provincial governments to fund youth housing programs across the country. *Limitation:* it is slow, and millions of people must organize to get decision makers to act.
>
> A strong answer also notes the lecture's point that each level fills a gap left by the one before it.

### Exercise 6 (Applied variation)

A student writes: "Social movements are just groups that want to make things better, so anti-abortion groups can't be a social movement." Using the definition of a social movement and the five types, explain what is wrong with this claim.

> [!success]- Answer key
> The definition says a social movement acts collectively to **promote or resist** change. A group doesn't have to push for something new to count. Among the five types, **resistance movements** exist to prevent or undo change that has already occurred, and the slides list anti-abortion groups and opposition to same-sex marriage as examples. Whether you agree with a movement's goals has nothing to do with whether it is a social movement.

### Exercise 7 (Challenge)

MADD started in the 1980s as a small group of parents whose children had been killed by drunk drivers. Today MADD Canada is a well-known national organization. Using this lecture, describe how MADD moved across the levels of social context. Then explain whether it still fits the lecture's description of a social movement as "not institutionalized and outside the political mainstream."

> [!success]- Answer key
> - **Micro:** each parent first dealt with grief inside their primary group (family and close friends).
> - **Mid-range:** grieving parents came together with others who had the same experience and goals, much like a self-help group, and then began working for community change like a grassroots group.
> - **Macro:** MADD grew into a social movement and began pressuring governments and the media to treat drunk driving as a social problem, leading to stricter impaired driving laws.
>
> **Does it still fit?** The lecture says social movements are not institutionalized **when they begin**. MADD fit that description at the start: ordinary parents outside the political mainstream. Over time it became organized and widely recognized, and it now behaves a lot like a special interest group that lobbies through the political system. A strong answer points out that movements can become more institutionalized as they succeed, which is why the definition says "when they begin".

> - What is social change (Kendall, 2000)? :: The alteration, modification, or transformation of public policy, culture, or social institutions over time.
> - What is the sociological imagination? :: The ability to see the relationship between an individual's experiences and the larger society.
> - What are the three levels of social context in the lecture's diagram, from inside to outside? :: Micro, macro, global.
> - Why can't social problems be solved at the individual level? :: Because they are more than personal troubles or private problems; their causes are in society.
> - Why do people often start dealing with a social problem in an individualized way? :: Because social institutions can't deal with it effectively, and political and business leaders are unwilling or unable to allocate the needed resources.
> - What do micro level solutions focus on? :: How individuals operate within small groups.
> - What is a primary group? :: A small, less-specialized group whose members have face-to-face, emotion-based interactions over a long period (e.g. family, close friends).
> - What kind of support does a primary group usually give for personal problems? :: Short-term support.
> - What do mid-range solutions focus on? :: How secondary groups and formal organizations can help individuals overcome issues such as drug addiction or domestic violence.
> - What is a secondary group? :: A more specialized, activity-oriented group with defined roles and expectations.
> - What do self-help groups bring together? :: Individuals who have experienced the same problem and have the same goals.
> - Name the two best-known self-help groups mentioned in the lecture. :: Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Narcotics Anonymous (NA).
> - What is a limitation of self-help groups? :: They help individuals cope, but a larger societal intervention is often needed to reduce the problems behind individual behaviours.
> - What are grassroots groups? :: Organizations started by ordinary people to deal with a perceived problem in their neighbourhood, city, province or territory, or nation.
> - According to the "deeper issue" quote, what is the crisis? :: People don't know how to come together to solve problems; too many people feel powerless.
> - What is a social movement? :: An organized group that acts collectively to promote or resist change through collective action.
> - What are social movements like when they begin? :: Not institutionalized and outside the political mainstream; they give outsiders a chance to be heard.
> - Give three examples of social movements from the lecture. :: Greenpeace, Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD), Canadian Peace Alliance.
> - What do macro level solutions focus on? :: Persuading large-scale social institutions, such as government and the media, to help remedy social problems.
> - What is a special interest (pressure) group? :: A political coalition of individuals or groups sharing a specific interest they want to protect or advance through the political system.
> - What are the four ways to categorize special interest groups? :: Issue focus; views of the present system of wealth and power; beliefs about elites; type of political action.
> - What is collective behaviour? :: Voluntary, often spontaneous activity of many people that typically violates dominant norms and values (e.g. demonstrations, riots).
> - What is civil disobedience? :: Non-violent action that seeks to change a policy or law by refusing to comply with it (e.g. sit-ins, strikes, marches, boycotts).
> - What is the difference between reform and revolutionary movements? :: Reform movements change a specific aspect of the social structure; revolutionary movements seek total change in society.
> - What do resistance movements do? :: Try to prevent or undo change that has already occurred (e.g. anti-abortion groups, opposition to same-sex marriage).
> - What do alternative movements seek? :: Limited change in some aspects of people's behaviour.
> - What do religious/expressive movements seek? :: To renew people through inner change; they often also push officials to pass laws reducing perceived social problems.
