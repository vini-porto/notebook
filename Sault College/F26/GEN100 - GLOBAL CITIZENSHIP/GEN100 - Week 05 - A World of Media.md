## 2. What is media?

> [!note] Definition: Mass media
> **[[Mass Media]]** is any medium designed to communicate messages to a **mass audience**.

A *medium* (plural: *media*) is a channel that carries a message: a TV station, a newspaper, a radio show, a website, an app. "Mass" means the message reaches a very large number of people at once. The image on the slide shows the main types: digital, social, news, audio, video and information media.

> [!note] Definition: Media literacy
> **[[Media Literacy]]** is the ability to **recognize**, **critically assess** and **make informed choices** about the messages contained in mass media forms.

Media literacy is like reading skill for media. Reading a news headline is easy; asking *who wrote this, why, who paid for it, and what was left out* takes media literacy.

> [!tip] Learning tip
> Media literacy has three verbs in its definition: recognize, assess, choose. If you can explain each one with an example (spot an ad disguised as news, check whether the source is reliable, decide whether to share it), you understand the definition.

## 3. Passive media

> [!note] Definition: Passive media
> **[[Passive Media]]** allows for **little or no direct input** into the content from the user.

- Most **traditional mass media** is passive: TV, magazines, radio, books, newspapers, etc.
- Passive media is **created and controlled by a few** and **consumed by the masses**.

When you watch the evening news, you can't change what the anchor says. A small group of producers and editors decide the content, and millions of people receive it. That one-way flow is what makes it passive.

## 4. Media framing

> [!note] Definition: Media framing
> **[[Media Framing]]** is presenting stories and issues according to the reality that the media wants the public (the receivers) to accept.

A frame works like a picture frame: it decides what is inside the picture and what is left out. The same event can look very different depending on how it is framed.

The slide gives three reasons media frame stories:

| Reason | What it means | Related term on the slide |
|---|---|---|
| Promote particular interests | The story is shaped to support a group, company or political side | Bias, [[Propaganda\|propaganda]] |
| Drive ratings | The story is made scary or shocking so more people watch | [[Moral Panic\|Moral panic]] |
| Fit a certain narrative or storyline | The story is shaped to match a simple, familiar plot | Convenient, entertainment |

A *moral panic* is a wave of public fear, often out of proportion, that something or some group threatens society's values or safety. Fear keeps people watching, which raises ratings.

> [!example] Same event, different frames
> A protest that blocks a highway can be framed as "Citizens demand action on climate" or as "Protesters cause traffic chaos." Both headlines describe the same event; each one pushes the audience toward a different reaction.

## 6. Sociological approaches to media

The lecture compares two ways sociologists look at mass media:

> [!important] Key idea
> For some, the mass media **promotes common values**, while others see it as a **tool to control people**.

The first view is functionalism; the second is conflict theory.

### Functionalism

> [!note] Functionalist view
> According to **[[Functionalism]]**, mass media **promotes common values and beliefs**. It does jobs (functions) that help society run smoothly.

The slide lists four functions:

| Function | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **[[Socialization]] function** | Helps individuals learn the culture, attitudes, values and norms needed to participate in society | Kids learn about manners, gender roles or national holidays from TV shows |
| **Surveillance function** | Gathers and spreads information for the population | The news reports a storm warning or an election result |
| **Correlation function** | Presents difficult, complex issues in a way most people can understand | A news explainer breaks down how interest rates affect your rent |
| **Entertainment function** | Allows people to... (the rest of this line is cut off on the slide) | Movies, music, sports broadcasts |

*Socialization* is the social process in which individuals learn the culture, attitudes, values and norms necessary to participate in society. Family and school socialize us too; the point here is that media does it as well.

> [!warning] Cut-off slide
> The entertainment function is cut off at the bottom of the slide. In sociology textbooks it usually means media lets people relax and escape from everyday stress. Check your textbook or class notes for the exact wording your instructor uses.

### Conflict theory

> [!note] Conflict theory view
> According to **[[Conflict Theory]]**, the **rich and powerful elites control the masses** through the mass media and **reinforce false consciousness**.

The slide asks: *What do we think this means?* In simple terms, the people who own the media also have the most money and power. They can use it to make everyone else accept a system that mostly benefits the rich, so ordinary people don't question it.

Key ideas from conflict theory:

- **News media reflect the dominant class's [[Ideology|ideology]].** An *ideology* is a set of beliefs about how society should work. The "dominant class" is the group with the most wealth and power.

> [!note] Definition: False consciousness
> **[[False Consciousness]]** is belief in and support of the system that oppresses you. It means not being aware of the class to which one belongs.

> [!example] False consciousness
> A low-wage worker strongly supports tax cuts for the very rich because TV keeps telling them it will create jobs, even if their own wages never rise. Conflict theorists would say the worker is supporting a system that works against their own interests.

> [!note] Definition: Propaganda
> **[[Propaganda]]** is the large-scale use of information that is created to appear truthful but is instead an **intentional manipulation of the facts** to promote a particular worldview, in order to influence the opinions and/or behaviours of the majority of the population.

> [!note] Definition: Propaganda model
> The **[[Propaganda Model]]** is the assertion that mainstream media companies will transmit content that reflects their **commercial interests**.

The propaganda model says a media company is a business first. It will avoid stories that hurt its owners or advertisers and push content that makes money. The idea comes from Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's book *Manufacturing Consent* ([Britannica: Noam Chomsky](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Noam-Chomsky)).

### Comparing the two approaches

| | Functionalism | Conflict theory |
|---|---|---|
| Main view of media | Promotes common values and beliefs | A tool the elites use to control the masses |
| Who benefits | Society as a whole | The rich and powerful (dominant class) |
| Key terms | Socialization, surveillance, correlation, entertainment functions | False consciousness, propaganda, propaganda model, media consolidation |
| Example reading of the news | Keeps people informed and connected | Spreads the ideas of the dominant class |

## 8. Assumptions underlying media literacy

The slide lists five assumptions that media literacy is built on:

| # | Assumption | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **All media are constructions** | Every message was made by someone who chose what to include, cut and highlight. Media is never a direct window on reality. |
| 2 | **Audiences negotiate meaning** | Each person interprets a message in their own way, based on their age, culture, experience and beliefs. |
| 3 | **Media have commercial interests** | Most media is a business that needs profit, usually from advertising or subscriptions. |
| 4 | **Media have social and political implications** | Media shapes how people see social groups, issues and politicians, and can affect how they vote or act. |
| 5 | **Each medium has a unique aesthetic form** | Every medium has its own style and tools: TV uses images and music, a podcast uses voice, a tweet uses short text. The form changes how the message feels. |

> [!example] Applying the five assumptions
> Take a 30-second fast-food ad. It was carefully built (construction). A teen might see it as fun, a parent as unhealthy (negotiated meaning). It exists to sell burgers (commercial interest). It can shape ideas about diet and body image (social implication). It uses bright colours, fast cuts and a catchy jingle that only work in video form (aesthetic form).

## 10. Contemporary media: the media revolution

> [!note] Definition: Viral media
> **[[Viral Media]]** is produced by **amateurs and professionals** and gains widespread popularity and distribution through the internet (usually short video clips).

The slide's image shows one large bird (the Twitter logo) passing a message to three birds, who each pass it to more birds, and so on. That is how something "goes viral": each person shares it with several others, so the audience grows very fast, like a virus spreading.

> [!important] Key idea
> Viral media shows the shift from passive to active media. An amateur with a phone can now reach millions of people, something that used to be possible only for TV networks and newspapers.

## 12. Digital meeting places

Online spaces (chats, forums, social platforms) can help people in several ways:

- People can **practice social skills** in a far less intimidating environment than face-to-face interactions.
- Interactions are **more focused** (for example, themed chats), and users share similar interests.
- They are a **safe place to experiment** with personal relationships.
- There are **few cultural or spatial barriers**. *Spatial* means related to space or distance, so people can connect across cities and countries.
- They are **beneficial for those with learning disabilities or social disorders**.

The slide asks: *Why would this platform be helpful for some individuals? What are the pros and cons?*

| Pros (from the slide) | Possible cons (to discuss) |
|---|---|
| Less intimidating than face-to-face | People may avoid building in-person skills |
| Focused interactions with people who share interests | Echo chambers: hearing only views you already agree with |
| Safe place to experiment with relationships | Fake identities, scams and online harassment |
| Few cultural or distance barriers | Misunderstandings without body language or tone |
| Helps people with learning disabilities or social disorders | Risk of isolation or overuse |

> [!tip] Learning tip
> The cons column above is not on the slide; it is there to help you answer the discussion question. On a test, use the five points from the slide as the "pros".

## 13. CNN and 24/7 news media

[[CNN]] was the first 24-hour TV news channel (launched in 1980). The lecture uses it as the example of the **24/7 news cycle**.

### 24 hours of news?

- Many journalists fear that in today's internet and 24/7 news cycles, **factual reporting is losing ground** to loud, splashy and fast **[[Sensationalism|sensational]] reporting**.
- There can be **no downtime or dead air**. CNN broadcasts stories that are sometimes **breaking and important**, and sometimes **unimportant and repetitive**.
- With so much airtime to fill, journalism is frequently transformed into **commentary and opinion**: a parade of **pundits** ("experts") dissect and debate the smallest details of the story.

*Dead air* is silence or an empty moment on a broadcast. A *pundit* is a commentator who gives opinions on TV, often presented as an expert.

### Breaking news?

When important breaking news happens, coverage is so immediate that there is little or no time for the traditional **mediation** of the stories (the normal process of checking, editing and selecting before something goes on air). This causes two problems:

1. **Reporters have little time to check facts thoroughly.**
   - Reported "facts" sometimes turn out to be wrong and must be corrected (refuted).
2. **Editors have little time to select and focus stories before they go on air.**
   - The larger and longer-term **context may be lost**.
   - Natural **reporting bias** can slip in more easily.

> [!warning] Common misconception
> "Live" or "breaking" does not mean "more accurate." Breaking news is often the *least* checked version of a story. Wait for follow-up reports before treating early details as facts.

## 14. The ethical challenges of a converged world

The slide uses the Spider-Man quote **"With great power comes great responsibility."**

- The internet, social media and other technology have given the **public the tools to produce mass media** at the click of a button.
- This power was **once controlled by only a few elites**.
- With this power comes a **greater level of individual responsibility**.
- We are **legally and ethically responsible** for our online behaviour.

A *converged world* is one where different media (TV, phone, newspaper, camera, internet) have merged into the same devices and platforms. Your phone is a camera, a TV, a newspaper and a broadcasting studio at once.

> [!example] Individual responsibility online
> Sharing a false rumour, posting a photo of someone without permission, or making threats in a comment section can all lead to real consequences: lost jobs, lawsuits, or criminal charges in serious cases such as harassment or defamation.

## 15. Journalism ethics

Professional journalists are expected to follow five ethical principles. See also the [Canadian Association of Journalists ethics guidelines](https://caj.ca/ethics-guidelines/).

| # | Principle | What the public expects |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Truth, honesty and impartiality** | Absolute honesty or complete lack of bias is impossible, but society assumes professional journalists are committed to reporting their stories **as honestly and impartially as possible**. |
| 2 | **Privacy** | Despite controversies around tabloid journalism, aggressive paparazzi and debates about who counts as a public figure, journalists should **fairly weigh the importance of a story and the public's right to know** against the equally important **right to individual privacy**. |
| 3 | **Confidentiality** | There is a push for laws protecting reporters from being forced to reveal confidential information or sources. Either way, it is generally accepted practice to **protect the identity of sources when asked to do so**. |
| 4 | **Profit and conflict of interest** | The news business is mainly commercially funded, but journalists are expected to **refuse bribes** and to **avoid even the appearance of a conflict of interest** in what they report and how they present it. |
| 5 | **Social responsibility** | Journalists are seen as working in the **general public interest**, so they should do their work in a way that does **not intentionally harm others**. |

Some terms:

- **Impartiality**: not taking sides.
- **Tabloid journalism**: sensational reporting focused on scandal, crime and celebrity gossip.
- **Paparazzi**: photographers who aggressively follow celebrities for pictures.
- **[[Conflict of Interest|Conflict of interest]]**: a situation where a person's private interests (money, friendships) could affect their professional judgment.

> [!example] Confidentiality
> A government employee tells a reporter about wrongdoing but asks to stay anonymous for fear of losing their job. Protecting that source lets the public learn about the wrongdoing and keeps future whistleblowers willing to come forward.

> [!example] Conflict of interest
> A reporter writes a positive review of a new car after the car company gave them a free vacation. Even if the review is honest, it *looks* bought. That appearance is what journalists are expected to avoid.

> [!important] Key idea
> Notice the link between journalism ethics and the earlier sections. Principle 4 pushes back against the commercial pressure described by the propaganda model, and principle 1 pushes back against media framing and the bias that slips into breaking news.

> - **Active media:** the teen created content, and other users shared it; the audience helped spread the message instead of just receiving it.
> - **Converged world:** a phone let the public produce mass media at the click of a button, a power once controlled by only a few elites. Traditional (passive) TV news then picked it up.
> - **Responsibility:** with that power comes individual responsibility. The teen is legally and ethically responsible for what they posted (for example, if it shows someone without consent or spreads false information).

### Exercise 7 (Challenge)

A friend says: "I only get my news from social media, so I'm not influenced by big media companies the way TV viewers are." Using the five **assumptions underlying media literacy**, media framing and the January 2020 social network chart, evaluate this claim.

> [!success]- Answer key
> A strong answer challenges the claim:
> - **All media are constructions:** social media posts are also made by someone who chose what to include and leave out.
> - **Media have commercial interests:** social platforms are businesses that earn money from ads, so their feeds are designed to keep people engaged. Framing to drive attention (similar to driving ratings) still happens.
> - **Media have social and political implications:** posts and feeds can shape views on politics and social groups.
> - **Consolidation online:** the chart shows that a few companies dominate (Facebook's owner also runs WhatsApp, Messenger and Instagram; several top networks are owned by Chinese companies). Big media still controls the platform even when users create the content.
> - **Audiences negotiate meaning:** the friend does interpret content personally, but that is true for TV viewers as well.
> - **Evaluation:** social media is more active than TV, but it does not escape commercial interests, framing or concentrated ownership. Media literacy is still needed.

### Exercise 8 (Challenge)

You are a social service worker designing an online peer-support group for newcomers to Canada who feel isolated. Using the **digital meeting places** slide, explain why an online group could help. Then identify two possible risks and suggest one rule for the group based on the ethics of a converged world or journalism ethics.

> [!success]- Answer key
> Answers vary. A strong answer:
> - **Benefits from the slide:** newcomers can practise social skills in a less intimidating setting than face-to-face; a themed group creates focused interaction among people with similar experiences; it is a safe place to build relationships; few cultural or spatial barriers (people from different neighbourhoods or countries of origin can join); it can help members with learning disabilities or social anxiety.
> - **Risks:** misunderstanding without tone or body language; scams or fake identities; misinformation about immigration rules; people relying only on online contact.
> - **Possible rules:** keep members' personal information confidential (linked to confidentiality and privacy); share only information from reliable sources (truth and honesty); no harassment (social responsibility and the point that we are legally and ethically responsible for online behaviour).

> - What is the surveillance function of media? :: Gathering and disseminating information for the population (for example, news).
> - What is the correlation function of media? :: Presenting difficult and complex issues in a way most people can understand.
> - How does conflict theory see mass media? :: As a tool rich and powerful elites use to control the masses and reinforce false consciousness.
> - What is false consciousness? :: Belief in and support of the system that oppresses you; not being aware of the class to which one belongs.
> - What is propaganda? :: Large-scale use of information made to appear truthful but intentionally manipulating facts to promote a worldview and influence the opinions or behaviours of most of the population.
> - What is the propaganda model? :: The assertion that mainstream media companies transmit content that reflects their commercial interests.
> - What is media consolidation? :: The concentration of ownership of news sources into the hands of fewer and fewer corporations.
