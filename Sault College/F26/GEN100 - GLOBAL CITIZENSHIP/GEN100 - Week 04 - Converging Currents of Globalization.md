## 2. What is globalization?

> [!note] Definition: Globalization
> **[[Globalization]]** is a process and a condition that involves the increasing interconnectedness of different parts of the world through common processes of **economic, environmental, political, and cultural change**.

The definition calls globalization both a *process* and a *condition*. As a process, the world keeps becoming more connected. As a condition, we already live in a connected world: the phone in your pocket, the food in your fridge and the news you read come from many different countries.

The definition names four areas where the connection happens:

| Area | What gets connected | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Trade, money, jobs, companies | Clothes sewn in Bangladesh and sold in Sault Ste. Marie |
| Environmental | Pollution, climate, resources | Emissions from one country add to global warming everywhere |
| Political | Governments, laws, agreements | Countries signing trade deals or joining the [[United Nations]] |
| Cultural | Food, music, language, values | The same streaming shows watched in Canada, Brazil and India |

> [!important] Key idea
> Globalization poses many challenges to conventional **[[State Sovereignty|state sovereignty]]** and to human populations.
>
> *State sovereignty* is a country's right to make its own decisions inside its own borders without outside control. When economies, problems and companies cross borders, a government cannot fully control what happens to its own people anymore. A factory closing in Ontario may be decided in a head office in another country.

## 3. Key players: TNCs and NGOs

### Transnational corporations (TNCs)

> [!note] Definition: Transnational corporation
> A **[[Transnational Corporations|transnational corporation (TNC)]]** is any corporation that is registered and operates in more than one country at a time. It is also called a **multinational corporation (MNC)**. A TNC has its **headquarters in one country** and operates wholly or partially owned **subsidiaries** in one or more other countries.

A *subsidiary* is a company owned (fully or partly) by another company. Walmart Canada, for example, is a subsidiary of Walmart, whose headquarters is in the United States.

The slide asks for examples of TNCs/MNCs and shows a collage of logos:

| Sector | Companies shown on the slide |
|---|---|
| Food and drink | McDonald's, Coca-Cola, Tim Hortons, General Mills, Molson Coors, Chiquita |
| Energy | Exxon, BP, Suncor Energy, Sinopec |
| Retail and consumer goods | Walmart, Nike, Apple, Firestone |
| Pharmaceuticals and agriculture | GlaxoSmithKline (GSK), Monsanto |
| Transportation and manufacturing | Bombardier |

> [!example] TNCs you might not expect
> Tim Hortons, Suncor and Bombardier are Canadian brands, but they still count as TNCs because they operate in more than one country. Sinopec is a Chinese state-owned oil company. TNCs come from many countries, not only the United States.

### Non-governmental organizations (NGOs)

> [!note] Definition: Non-governmental organization
> A **[[Non-Governmental Organizations|non-governmental organization (NGO)]]** is a voluntary group of individuals or organizations, usually not affiliated with any government, that is formed to **provide services** or to **advocate a public policy**. Although some NGOs are for-profit corporations, the vast majority are non-profit organizations.

Examples from the slide:

- **[[Red Cross|Red Cross / Red Crescent]]**: emergency relief and humanitarian aid ([IFRC](https://www.ifrc.org))
- **[[Greenpeace]]**: environmental campaigns ([Greenpeace](https://www.greenpeace.org))
- **[[Human Rights Watch]]**: investigates and reports on human rights abuses ([HRW](https://www.hrw.org))

The slide also asks: *Why are these important in SSW?* (SSW most likely refers to Social Service Work.) Some points to think about:

- NGOs often deliver services that governments don't provide, such as shelters, food banks, disaster relief and refugee support. Social service workers may work for NGOs or refer clients to them.
- NGOs work across borders, so they can respond to global problems (refugees, disasters, human rights abuses) that a single government can't solve alone.
- NGOs advocate for vulnerable groups, which is also part of social service work.

| | TNC | NGO |
|---|---|---|
| Main goal | Profit | Services or advocacy |
| Government ties | Private business | Usually not affiliated with any government |
| Profit status | For-profit | Usually non-profit |
| Examples | Nike, Walmart, Suncor | Red Cross, Greenpeace, Human Rights Watch |

## 4. Seeds of globalization

The **world system begins in the late 1400s**, with **economic gain** as the driver of the European voyages of discovery. European kingdoms sent ships to find new trade routes and wealth (spices, gold, silver, land).

Several factors helped this world system begin, including:

- **Advances in shipbuilding**, which made long ocean voyages possible.
- The **replacement of [[Feudalism|feudalism]] with [[Merchant Capitalism|merchant capitalism]]**.

> [!note] Definition: Feudalism
> **[[Feudalism]]** was a system in which people were given land and protection by people of higher rank, and worked and fought for them in return.

Under feudalism, wealth came from land and stayed local. Under merchant capitalism, wealth came from **trade**: merchants bought goods in one place and sold them somewhere else for profit. That gave people a reason to sail around the world.

## 5. Imperialism, colonialism and capitalism

Between the **15th century and the middle of the 18th century**, **England, France, the Netherlands, Portugal and Spain** built empires in the **Americas, India and the East Indies**.

The **first multinational corporations** were born in Europe in the **17th century**:

| Company | Year founded |
|---|---|
| [[British East India Company]] | 1600 |
| [[Dutch East India Company]] | 1602 |
| [[Hudson's Bay Company]] | 1670 |

> [!example] A Canadian link
> The Hudson's Bay Company was given control over a huge area of what is now Canada for the fur trade. It shows how early "multinationals" were tied to colonial expansion: the company traded, governed territory and worked with the British Crown at the same time.

### Colonialism vs. imperialism

> [!note] Definition: Colonialism
> **[[Colonialism]]** is the establishment and maintenance of political, economic and legal domination over a separate and foreign society (its land and people).

> [!note] Definition: Imperialism
> **[[Imperialism]]** is the state policy, practice or advocacy of extending power, especially by direct territorial acquisition or by gaining political and economic control of other areas.

In colonialism, **citizens of the colonizing nation settle in the foreign land** to exert political, legal and economic dominance.

| | Imperialism | Colonialism |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A policy or practice of extending a state's power | The actual domination of a foreign society and its land |
| Settlers needed? | Not always; control can be political or economic | Yes, citizens settle in the foreign land |
| Scope | The broader idea or goal | One way of carrying out that goal |

> [!tip] Learning tip
> Think of imperialism as the goal (extend your power over other areas) and colonialism as one method (send your own people to settle there and run things). For more detail, see [Britannica: Colonialism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/colonialism).

## 6. Decolonization and neocolonialism

> [!note] Definition: Decolonization
> **[[Decolonization]]** is the process by which colonies become independent of the colonizing country: the return of sovereignty to the original inhabitants of colonized states.

By the **1960s and 1970s**, decolonization was almost complete. Most former colonies in Africa and Asia had become independent countries.

> [!note] Definition: Neocolonialism
> **[[Neocolonialism]]** is the control of less-developed countries by developed countries through **indirect means**, such as transnational corporations, foreign aid and intergovernmental organizations (IGOs).

*Neo* means "new". Neocolonialism is a new form of control: no soldiers or settlers are needed. A powerful country or company can shape a poorer country's economy through investment, loans with conditions attached, or aid that comes with strings.

> [!warning] Common misconception
> Independence on paper does not always mean full control in practice. A country can be politically independent while big decisions about its economy are still made by foreign corporations or lenders. That gap is what the term neocolonialism describes.

### Neocolonialism and corporations

- TNCs can **relocate operations** to areas with relatively **low regulations** and **cheaper labour and land**.
- TNCs **pay billions to governments in taxes**.
- Corporations also **bring many jobs** to the areas they relocate to.
- In **2016**, the world's **top 100 economies** were **31 countries and 69 corporations** (World Bank figures cited on the slide).

The last point shows how big corporations have become: most of the 100 largest "economies" in the world are companies, not countries. A corporation with more money than a government has a lot of bargaining power over that government.

> [!important] Key idea
> The slide shows both sides. TNCs bring jobs and tax money, and they also move to places where labour is cheap and rules are weak. Whether that counts as development or as neocolonialism depends on who benefits and who has the power.

### Free trade and NAFTA

> [!note] Definition: NAFTA
> The **[[North American Free Trade Agreement|North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)]]** was a **1994** agreement between **Canada, the United States and Mexico** that eliminated **tariffs** and other trade barriers between the three countries.

A *tariff* is a tax on imported goods. Removing tariffs makes it cheaper to sell products across the border.

> [!tip] Current context
> NAFTA was replaced in 2020 by the **Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA)**, called USMCA in the United States. The idea (reducing trade barriers between the three countries) is the same. See [Government of Canada: CUSMA](https://www.international.gc.ca/trade-commerce/trade-agreements-accords-commerciaux/agr-acc/cusma-aceum/index.aspx?lang=eng).

## 8. Positives and negatives

- **Not all world regions have shared in the benefits of globalization.** [[Trickle-down Economics|Trickle-down economics]] is either not happening or not happening for all people.
- **Economic interconnectivity can also increase economic vulnerability.**

> [!note] Definition: Trickle-down economics
> **Trickle-down economics** is the idea that if businesses and wealthy people do well (through lower taxes, more trade, more investment), the benefits will eventually "trickle down" to everyone else as jobs and higher wages.

The lecture's point is that this promise has not come true for everyone. Some regions and people got richer; others were left behind.

> [!example] Interconnection as vulnerability
> When economies depend on each other, a problem in one place spreads. The 2008 financial crisis started in the US housing market and pushed many countries into recession. During COVID-19, factory shutdowns in one country caused shortages of products around the world.

## 9. Economic globalization

Since the end of **World War II**, legal and other barriers to international trade, like **tariffs** (taxes on imports), have been removed.

| Proponents argue it brings | Critics argue it brings |
|---|---|
| Efficiency | Widespread inequality |
| Lower prices | Homogeneity |
| Higher profits | |
| Diversity of products | |

> [!note] Definition: Homogeneity
> **Homogeneity** is the quality or state of being all the same. Critics worry that globalization makes places look alike: the same stores, the same brands and the same food in every city.

## 10. The global workplace

> [!note] Definition: Sweatshop
> **[[Sweatshops]]** are manufacturing facilities where workers are physically or mentally ill-treated, typically confined in crowded conditions for long hours at a very low rate of pay.

With increased interconnectedness, multinational corporations can **relocate in search of cheaper labour, land and capital**.

> [!note] Definition: Outsourcing
> **[[Outsourcing]]** is the reorganization of labour within and between societies. It is a strategy, common since the **1980s**, in which corporations in **1st World** nations move **non-management operations** to external sites, often in **3rd World** locations, where land and labour are comparatively cheap and regulation is low.

"First World" and "Third World" are older terms. Today people usually say *developed* (or high-income) and *developing* (or low-income) countries.

> [!example] Outsourcing and sweatshops in the news
> In 2013 the Rana Plaza building in Bangladesh collapsed and killed more than 1,100 garment workers. The factories inside made clothes for well-known Western brands. The disaster showed how outsourcing to low-regulation locations can lead to sweatshop conditions. See [BBC: Rana Plaza](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-22476774).

> [!warning] Common mistake
> Outsourcing usually moves **non-management** work. The head office, executives and decision making normally stay in the home country, while manufacturing, call centres or data entry move abroad.

## 11. Effects of globalization on culture

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| **Human rights** (spreading values) | **Consumerism** (spreading values) |
| **Diversity in diet** | **Changes in diet ("McWorld")** |
| **Cultural exchange** may lead to understanding, innovation, diversity, etc. | **Loss and westernization** of language and other culture (export of culture from **core to periphery**) |
| **Role of women** | **Role of women** |
| **Hybridization** | **Hybridization** |

Some terms from the table:

- **[[Consumerism]]**: a set of values centred on buying and owning more things.
- **"McWorld"**: a nickname for a world where fast food and global brands replace local food and culture.
- **Core and periphery**: the *core* is the rich, powerful countries; the *periphery* is the poorer countries. Culture usually flows from the core outward, which can push aside local languages and traditions. This is called **westernization**.
- **[[Cultural Hybridization|Hybridization]]**: when cultures mix and create something new.

> [!important] Key idea
> Role of women and hybridization appear in **both** columns, because the same change can be good or bad depending on the case. Globalization can spread ideas about women's equality and create factory jobs that give women their own income; those same jobs can also be low-paid and exploitative. Hybridization can create new music, food and art; it can also water down traditional cultures.

> [!example] Hybridization
> Korean-Mexican tacos, K-pop songs mixing Korean and English, and poutine pizza are all hybrid products of cultures meeting.

## 12. Effects of globalization on people

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Increase in migration of people, **legally**, temporarily and permanently | Increase in migration of people, **illegally**, temporarily and permanently |
| Brings **jobs** and other radical changes to quality of life | **Job loss** and other radical changes to quality of life |
| | **Indigenous peoples often displaced** from their lands (the rest of this row is cut off on the slide) |

The positive and negative sides mirror each other: migration and job changes happen either way, and whether they help or hurt depends on who you are and where you are.

> [!example] Displacement of Indigenous peoples
> Large mining, logging, oil or dam projects run for global markets often take place on Indigenous lands. Communities can be pushed off land they have lived on for generations or lose access to hunting, fishing and sacred sites.

## 13. Effects of globalization on politics

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| **Not restricted by boundaries**: political cooperation between countries | **Weakened political power of individual countries** due to economic reliance on other countries and multinational corporations (MNCs) |
| **Weakened political power of despotic leaders** due to (a) stronger local and separatist (the rest of this row is cut off on the slide) | **Weakened political power of individual countries** due to increased power of regional economic and (the rest of this row is cut off on the slide) |

A **despotic** leader is a ruler with absolute power who often uses it cruelly (a dictator). Globalization can weaken dictators because people can communicate, organize and see how others live.

Weakened national power appears on **both** sides. It is good when it limits dictators. It is bad when an elected government cannot protect its own citizens because it depends on other countries, large corporations or regional economic blocs (for example the European Union).

> [!example] Politics across borders
> During the Arab Spring (2010 to 2012), protesters in Tunisia and Egypt used social media to organize, and long-ruling leaders were forced out. That is an example of global communication weakening despotic leaders.

## 14. Effects of globalization on the environment

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| **International cooperation** in solving or mitigating global environmental problems: global warming, pollution (all types), deforestation, etc. | **Destruction of local ecosystems**: removing natural resources and creating manufacturing sites |
| | **Global environmental** problems (the rest of this row is cut off on the slide) |

*Mitigating* means reducing how bad something is.

> [!example] Environmental cooperation
> The [[Paris Agreement]] (2015) is a global deal in which almost every country agreed to cut greenhouse gas emissions to limit global warming. No single country could solve climate change alone. See [UN: The Paris Agreement](https://www.un.org/en/climatechange/paris-agreement).

## 15. Effects of globalization on Canada

- **[[Brain Drain|"Brain drain"]]**: the most educated individuals are often recruited abroad.
- Some **Canadian-based multinational corporations, owners and investors benefit** financially from increased global trade, lower labour costs, etc.
- **Loss of manufacturing and other jobs** to foreign countries where labour and other costs are cheaper.
- Canada welcomes **250,000 immigrants per year** (slide figure).
- Canada has an **official [[Multiculturalism|multicultural]] policy**.
- Canada is a **nation of immigrants**: **28% of Canadians will be foreign born by 2031** (slide projection).

> [!note] Definition: Brain drain
> **Brain drain** is when highly educated or skilled people (doctors, engineers, researchers) leave their country to work somewhere else, often for higher pay. For Canada, this often means losing talent to the United States.

> [!warning] Numbers have changed
> The 250,000 per year figure is older. Canada's immigration targets in the mid 2020s were much higher, in the range of 400,000 to 485,000 permanent residents a year, before being lowered for 2025 onward. For the test, learn the number on the slide, but know that real numbers change. See [Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca) for current data.

> [!tip] Canada wins and loses
> Notice that Canada appears on both sides of globalization. Canadian investors and companies gain from cheaper labour abroad, while Canadian factory workers lose jobs to those same cheaper locations.

## 16. More arguments in favour of globalization

1. A **logical and inevitable expression of [[Capitalism|capitalism]]** that will benefit all nations and all people.
2. Allows **flow of capital to poorer areas**.
3. Encourages the **spread of technologies and ideas**.
4. Encourages **efficiency** (through competition).
5. **Geographic specialization** leads to an efficient world economy.
6. Potential for **[[Economic Convergence|economic convergence]]**: poorer countries will eventually "catch up".

*Geographic specialization* means each region produces what it is best at or can make most cheaply (for example, Canada exporting lumber and wheat while importing electronics), and everyone trades.

## 17. More critiques of globalization

1. Globalization is **not a "natural" process**. It is the product of economic policy and capitalist countries, a form of **recolonization / neocolonialism**.
2. The **gap between rich and poor is growing**.
3. It favours the **free market over localized, sustainable activities**.
4. **Exploitation of resources and people**.
5. International policies promote **cutting social programs and education** (the future) to finance the present.
6. The so-called **"free market" doesn't exist**: past and present governments protect and support industries.
7. It **worsens environmental destruction and climate change**.

### Comparing the two sides

| Topic | In favour | Critique |
|---|---|---|
| Is it natural? | Logical and inevitable expression of capitalism | Not natural; a product of policy and capitalist countries |
| Rich and poor | Capital flows to poorer areas; poorer countries will "catch up" | The gap between rich and poor is growing |
| Markets | Competition creates efficiency | The "free market" doesn't really exist; governments protect industries |
| Local economies | Geographic specialization makes the world economy efficient | Free market wins over local, sustainable activities |
| People and resources | Spreads technology and ideas | Exploits resources and people |
| Environment | (Section 14: international cooperation) | Worsens environmental destruction and climate change |

> [!important] Key idea
> Both sides agree globalization changes the world. They disagree about **who benefits** and whether it is a natural, unavoidable process or a set of choices made by powerful countries and corporations. When you answer test questions, show both sides.

For data on global inequality and trade, see [Our World in Data: Globalization](https://ourworldindata.org/trade-and-globalization).

### Exercise 4 (Applied variation)

> - **Sweatshop risk:** with weak labour laws, the new factory could become a sweatshop, with crowded conditions, long hours and very low pay.
> - **Neocolonialism:** a corporation from a developed country is shaping a less-developed country's economy and working conditions through indirect means.
> - **Who is affected:** Ontario workers lose their jobs (the "loss of manufacturing jobs" effect on Canada). Owners and investors gain from lower costs. Workers in the new country get jobs, which the lecture lists as a benefit, but may face exploitation. A strong answer shows both sides.

### Exercise 5 (Applied variation)

### Exercise 6 (Applied variation)

Explain how **weakened political power of individual countries** can be a positive effect of globalization in one situation and a negative effect in another. Use the politics table.

> [!success]- Answer key
> - **Positive:** when the leader is despotic. Stronger local and separatist forces, plus global communication, can weaken a dictator's grip (for example, the Arab Spring).
> - **Negative:** when an ordinary country cannot make its own decisions because it depends economically on other countries and MNCs, or because regional economic blocs have gained power. Citizens lose control over policies that affect them, which connects to the challenge to state sovereignty in the definition of globalization.

### Exercise 7 (Challenge)

A classmate says: "Globalization is just how capitalism naturally works. Eventually every country will be rich." Using the lecture's arguments in favour and its critiques, write a short response that evaluates this claim.

> [!success]- Answer key
> A strong answer covers both sides:
> - **Support for the claim:** the arguments in favour say globalization is a logical and inevitable expression of capitalism, capital flows to poorer areas, and economic convergence means poorer countries will eventually "catch up". Spreading technology and efficiency through competition also help.
> - **Against the claim:** critics say globalization is not natural; it results from economic policy made by capitalist countries (a form of neocolonialism). The gap between rich and poor is growing, trickle-down economics has not worked for everyone, the "free market" doesn't really exist because governments protect industries, and resources and people are exploited.
> - **Evaluation:** the claim treats convergence as certain, but the lecture only calls it a *potential*. Since not all regions have shared in the benefits, the claim is at least partly contradicted by the evidence in the lecture.
